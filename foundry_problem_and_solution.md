# Foundry — Problem Statement and Proposed Solution

---

## Problem Statement

Engineering colleges produce hundreds of student projects every year — weekend builds, mini projects, and final year projects. Yet almost none of this work survives in any useful form.

Final year projects are submitted, bound, and shelved in a physical library where they are never opened again. Smaller projects live on personal hard drives, forgotten GitHub repositories, or WhatsApp groups that get archived when a semester ends. There is no institutional memory. Every batch starts from scratch.

This creates three compounding problems:

**Junior students have no reference point.** A first or second year student who wants to build something has no way of knowing what their seniors built, how they built it, what worked, or what failed. They don't know what a weekend project looks like versus a final year project. They don't know which ideas have already been tried. Without that context, students either pick the same generic ideas year after year — attendance systems, library management tools, hostel management portals — or they underscope their work because they have no benchmark to aim at.

**Senior work has no continuity.** A 2021 batch built a smart attendance system with basic features. A 2023 batch built the exact same thing independently, without knowing the prior work existed. Neither team benefits from the other. The 2021 project's limitations — which could have become the 2023 team's starting point — were never documented anywhere findable. Good ideas are reinvented instead of extended.

**Faculty and interviewers see the same projects every cycle.** Evaluators have no visibility into what students built outside of formal submissions. Interviewers see the same five project types because students have no inspiration to go beyond them. Innovation is expected but the conditions for it don't exist — students cannot innovate on top of work they cannot see.

There is also a secondary problem inside active projects. Teams building something over weeks or months have no shared space to think. They coordinate over WhatsApp, plan on paper, and lose context between sessions. When the project becomes a post at the end of a semester, the reasoning behind decisions — why PostgreSQL over MongoDB, why a feature was dropped, how the problem statement evolved — is gone. Only the final output survives.

## Proposed Solution

**Foundry** is a college project archival, collaboration, and evaluation platform built specifically for engineering students and faculty at NTTF NEC Bangalore.

The core idea is that projects should be shared throughout a student's academic life, not only at graduation. Foundry gives every project a public post — a persistent record that includes what was built, who built it, what technology was used, and where it sits in the lineage of every related project that came before it.

### Living Archive

Students post projects at any stage — a weekend build after Saturday, a mini project mid-semester, a final year project at graduation. Every post is timestamped, tagged with department and semester, and permanently discoverable by anyone at the college. A junior student browsing the platform sees not just final polished work but a realistic range of what their peers build at every level. The archive grows every semester rather than resetting.

### Project Lineage

Every project on Foundry can be forked. When a student builds on or improves an existing project, they fork the original post and write a rationale explaining what they changed and why. This creates a directed lineage — a living family tree of every idea and its descendants across years and departments. A student who built an attendance system in 2022 can watch their project evolve through three generations of forks by 2025 students who added face recognition, offline sync, and a mobile interface. The original work doesn't disappear — it becomes the root of something larger.

### AI-Assisted Grading with Human Approval

Faculty define rubrics in plain language. When a student submits a project for grading, the platform automatically analyses the code and documentation against the rubric and produces a structured first-pass grade with written justification per criterion. Faculty review this, modify any criteria they disagree with (with a reason), and approve. The grade is released to the student only after explicit faculty approval. No grade is ever generated by AI alone. This reduces the mechanical load on faculty while keeping them in full control of every evaluation decision.

### Collaboration Rooms

Teams working on a project can create a persistent Collaboration Room — a shared workspace that exists independent of any post. The room is project-aware from the moment it is created: it shows the team's problem statement, current stage, a three-question Blueprint for shared thinking, an auto-compiled Build Journey timeline, and a Kanban board for task tracking. For teams that want synchronous sessions, a Live Space opens the room into a 2D environment with proximity-based audio and video and a shared whiteboard.

Crucially, nothing about the room is mandatory. Teams that prefer WhatsApp and in-person meetings are not disadvantaged — every other platform feature works fully without a room being opened. The room is an option for teams that want a dedicated project space, not a requirement.

### Idea Hub

A dedicated tab surfaces two types of content: unbuilt Open Ideas that anyone at the college can claim and build, and Feature Requests on existing published projects. A student who built a minimal version of something can receive community suggestions for what to add next. Accepting a Feature Request automatically creates a fork, preserving the lineage chain. Auto-generated suggestions (labelled clearly as AI-produced) surface improvement ideas that match what is already missing from a project's feature set.

### Cross-Department Visibility

The feed shows projects across all departments. A computer science student looking for help with embedded systems can find a student in the electronics department. A mechanical student who needs a software dashboard for a hardware prototype can find a frontend developer across the aisle. The institutional wall between departments, which normally means students never meet outside their own cohort, becomes permeable through the platform.

### What Foundry Is Not

Foundry is not a learning management system. It does not replace attendance tracking, timetables, or any existing college infrastructure. It is not a grading portal — grading is one feature among many, and the platform is useful to students who are never graded through it at all. It is not a social network built for engagement — there are no algorithms optimising for time-on-site. It is a professional archive and collaboration tool that happens to be built for students.

---

## Who It Serves

**Students** — primarily, as the creators and consumers of the archive. Every student benefits from the lineage browser regardless of whether they post their own work.

**Faculty** — as evaluators, with AI assistance that reduces workload while keeping them in control of every grade decision.

**Alumni** — as passive contributors who can view, comment on, and post ideas to the Idea Hub after graduation, keeping institutional knowledge flowing from seniors to juniors naturally.

---

## Why Now

The problem has always existed. What has changed is that the tools to solve it — large language models capable of structured code analysis, vector similarity for plagiarism detection, real-time collaborative infrastructure, and WebRTC for proximity audio — are now accessible to a small student team without enterprise-level resources. Foundry is built on DigitalOcean infrastructure with open-source tooling, designed to run within the budget of a college pilot deployment.

The other thing that has changed is that faculty willingness to mandate platform usage is the difference between a tool students optionally ignore and an archive that actually fills up. With institutional backing, the archive becomes valuable from the first semester. Without it, it takes years. Foundry is designed to earn that backing by solving a problem faculty feel directly — seeing the same project ideas every cycle — not by asking them to change how they teach.

---

*Foundry is a final year project by a five-member team at NTTF NEC Bangalore, built over 12 months as a working platform for the college itself.*
